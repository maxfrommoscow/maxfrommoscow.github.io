Environmental claims after 27 September 2026: what furniture and building-product suppliers can still say

The rules are written for consumer communication, but they reach every supplier whose words end up on a showroom tag or a product page. The Italian and German press, a furniture laboratory and a building-products association explain where the line now runs.

7 min read·Updated Oct 20, 2026

In short

  • From 27 September 2026, generic claims such as 'eco-friendly' are allowed only with recognised excellent environmental performance — the EU Ecolabel or an officially recognised EN ISO 14024 type I ecolabel.
  • Claims of climate neutrality based on offsetting are banned, and a sustainability label must rest on a certification scheme or come from a public authority.
  • Specific, documented facts remain the safe way to speak: Rushil holds FSC and PEFC chain-of-custody certification and EN 15804+A2 EPDs for thin and compact HPL, valid until 29 June 2031.

How this review was put together

This article draws on the text of Directive (EU) 2024/825; on the Italian safety and environment magazine PuntoSicuro, which reported the European Commission's 2026 Q&A on the directive; on two technical notes from the furniture laboratory CATAS; on the German chemical-industry magazine CHEManager, on Germany's implementation; and on a guidance note from GEV, the German association behind the EMICODE emissions label for building products. Each point is attributed, and the sources are listed at the end.

What changes on 27 September 2026

Directive (EU) 2024/825 — "empowering consumers for the green transition" — amends the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, which governs business-to-consumer communication, and the Consumer Rights Directive. Member states must apply the new rules from 27 September 2026. CATAS notes that Italy transposed them with Legislative Decree 30/2026; CHEManager reports that Germany amended its unfair competition law (UWG), published in the Federal Law Gazette on 19 February 2026.

The core is a set of practices added to the blacklist — banned in all circumstances, without any need to show harm:

  • Displaying a sustainability label that is not based on a certification scheme or established by public authorities.
  • Making a generic environmental claim without being able to demonstrate recognised excellent environmental performance relevant to the claim.
  • Making an environmental claim about the entire product or business when it concerns only one aspect of it.
  • Claiming, on the basis of greenhouse-gas offsetting, that a product has a neutral, reduced or positive impact on the environment.
  • Presenting requirements that the law imposes on all products in a category as a distinctive feature of one offer.

What counts as a generic claim

An environmental claim is any non-mandatory message that states or implies a positive or zero impact, a lower impact than others, or an improvement over time — in text, pictures or symbols, and including brand, company or product names. It is generic when it is not on a sustainability label and its specification is not given clearly and prominently on the same medium. The directive's recitals name examples: environmentally friendly, eco-friendly, green, nature's friend, ecological, climate friendly, carbon friendly, energy efficient, biodegradable, biobased.

Such a claim is allowed only with recognised excellent environmental performance, which the directive defines as compliance with the EU Ecolabel, with an officially recognised EN ISO 14024 type I ecolabel, or with top performance under other Union law. PuntoSicuro cites the Commission's own contrast: "climate-friendly packaging" is generic; "100 % of the energy used to produce this packaging comes from renewable sources" is specific — provided the company can document it.

Brand names, leaves and water drops

CATAS summarised three answers from the Commission's Q&A. Brand and product names fall within the directive, because the definition of an environmental claim includes them — though the word "green" in a name, or the colour green, is not a claim in itself unless the context leads consumers to expect an environmental benefit; it is judged case by case. Water drops, green leaves and other natural symbols on packaging can be read as implicit claims or labels. And social characteristics — working conditions, wages, safety, human rights, inclusion — are covered too.

CATAS adds a caution: the Q&A is the Commission's preliminary view, not an official position; only the EU Court of Justice interprets the directive.

Business-to-business suppliers are not off the hook

According to PuntoSicuro's reading of the Q&A, the directive targets business-to-consumer communication before, during and after a sale; mandatory reports under the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive are outside it — unless a company lifts statements from them into its advertising. The same Q&A, the magazine reports, expects distributors, retailers and buyers to demand proof from their suppliers, and leaves no grace period for products already on the market if they are sold to consumers.

GEV's note for building-product makers draws the line from the other side: the blacklist does not bite where products are not intended for consumers and cannot reach them, but misleading practices towards other market participants are also unfair, and statements about durability, repairability or recyclability can mislead. For a panel supplier the practical point is simple: what goes on a data sheet often ends up, word for word, on a furniture maker's product page.

Labels after September

A sustainability label must now rest on a certification scheme or come from a public authority. The directive defines the scheme: third-party verification, open on transparent and non-discriminatory terms, requirements developed with experts and stakeholders, procedures for withdrawing the label, and monitoring by a third party whose competence and independence rest on recognised standards. Self-declared logos no longer qualify.

GEV applied this to its own label: it revised the EMICODE rules and considers the label compliant where a valid licence under the updated rules exists — but it stresses that the label does not entitle anyone to general environmental claims beyond what the EMICODE testing shows.

Where an EPD fits

An Environmental Product Declaration reports measured life-cycle results for a declared unit. Quoting a figure from it, with its unit and scope, is a specific statement rather than an adjective — the kind of substantiated information the directive points towards.

CATAS's June 2026 FAQ explains what to check before quoting one. An EPD needs at least one year of representative production data. One study can cover a family of products, reported as an average product, a representative product such as the best seller, or the worst product of the group — so a figure may be better or worse than the true value for a particular thickness or decor unless the EPD reports the worst case.

Reading this against the Rushil range

This site states what is certified, what is tested and what is not claimed. It makes no carbon-neutral, zero-impact or fully recyclable claim. The SEC Green Label was removed when the certificate expired on 16 August 2026, pending renewal. Rushil holds FSC and PEFC chain-of-custody certification, and Environmental Product Declarations to EN 15804+A2 cover thin decorative HPL (EPD-IES-0032917:001) and compact HPL (EPD-IES-0032915:001), valid until 29 June 2031.

If you resell or specify these panels, quote the document — the certificate, the test report, the EPD figure with its declared unit — rather than a summary adjective.

Common questions

Does the directive apply to business-to-business sales?+−

It amends consumer law and, according to the Commission's Q&A as reported by PuntoSicuro, targets communication to consumers. GEV notes that misleading practices towards other businesses are still unfair, and B2B claims often reach consumers through the customer.

Can a product still be called eco-friendly?+−

Only with recognised excellent environmental performance — the EU Ecolabel or an officially recognised EN ISO 14024 type I ecolabel — or with the specification stated clearly and prominently on the same medium.

Can we say a product is climate neutral because we offset its emissions?+−

No. Claims of a neutral, reduced or positive greenhouse-gas impact based on offsetting are on the blacklist from 27 September 2026.
