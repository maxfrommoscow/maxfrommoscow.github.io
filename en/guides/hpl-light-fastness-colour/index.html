Will the colour last? Light fastness of HPL compared with wood

Wood changes colour in light: it yellows, darkens or fades, even indoors under ordinary lamps. For many uses that is part of its charm. Where a colour has to stay as specified, a printed HPL decor is built to hold it. Here is what the research on wood shows, how light fastness is tested, and what Rushil's laminates achieve.

6 min read·Updated Oct 23, 2026

In short

  • Wood changes colour in light, even indoors: researchers saw visible change in birch, oak, spruce and pine under ordinary lamps, and yellowing dominated in a 2026 study of 40 species.
  • An HPL decor is a printed paper sealed under cured melamine. Rushil's TOPAZ reports 5 on the grey scale — no change — and 6 on the blue wool scale, against requirements of 4–5 and 4.
  • Outdoors, KLADS keeps appearance grade 5 and colour 4–5 after 3 000 hours of artificial weathering, against requirements of 4 and 3.

How this guide was put together

This guide draws on two studies of light-induced discolouration of wood, one from the Latvian State Institute of Wood Chemistry in Riga and one, published in 2026, covering 40 species; on the ICDLI's technical leaflet on HPL; and on Rushil's data sheets for Decorative HPL, TOPAZ and KLADS.

Wood changes colour, even indoors

Researchers at the Latvian State Institute of Wood Chemistry exposed birch, oak, spruce and pine to LED, incandescent and fluorescent lamps. Visible changes of colour appeared in all of them at relatively low doses of light, which the authors read as a high sensitivity of wood to ordinary artificial lighting. The hardwoods held their colour better than the softwoods; incandescent and fluorescent lamps caused more change in the long run, while LEDs caused faster change at first, cool 6500 K LEDs most of all, and the shade kept shifting as exposure built up.

A study published in September 2026 exposed 40 wood species to UV-B light for 240 hours. Yellowing dominated the colour change, and the species fell into four groups by how stable they were. For conservators the lesson is to choose wood with light in mind; for specifiers it is that a timber surface will not stay the colour of the sample.

Why a printed decor behaves differently

In HPL the decor is a pigmented or printed paper sealed under cured melamine resin, which the ICDLI describes as transparent and lightfast. The industry body draws two conclusions: the colour of a print is more lightfast than natural veneer or wood, and a print is identical over millions of square metres rather than varying from batch to batch like paint.

That is the point of a wood decor: the grain and warmth of timber, with a colour designed to stay where the designer put it.

How light fastness is tested

EN 438-2 tests light fastness by exposing a sample to a xenon-arc lamp and comparing exposed and unexposed areas against reference scales: the grey scale, on which 5 means no change, and, on some data sheets, the blue wool scale, on which higher numbers mean greater light fastness.

Rushil's data sheets report 4 to 5 on the grey scale for Decorative HPL, meeting the stated requirement of 4 to 5, and for TOPAZ 5 on the grey scale and 6 on the blue wool scale, against requirements of 4 to 5 and 4. These are test results, not a declaration of conformity.

Outdoors: KLADS

On a façade, light comes with heat, rain and ultraviolet. KLADS, Rushil's exterior compact, uses a décor paper with light fastness above 5 under a UV-protective film more than 50 µm thick. Its data sheet reports, from EN 438-2, grade 5 for appearance and 4 to 5 for colour on the grey scale after 3,000 hours of artificial weathering, and the same after 1,500 hours of UV exposure, against requirements of 4 and 3.

Results apply to the decors tested, so ask for the data on the decor you choose; for façades, check a sample in daylight on site.

What this means for a project

Wood is beautiful, and it changes. Where a colour has to stay as specified, on a wall that catches the sun through a window, on a counter under shop lighting or on a façade, a laminate decor is made to hold it, and Rushil's results put its laminates at or near the top of the light fastness scales. Data sheets and samples are available on request.

Common questions

Does HPL fade in sunlight?+−

HPL decors are tested for light fastness under a xenon-arc lamp. Rushil's TOPAZ data sheet reports 5 on the grey scale, where 5 means no change, and 6 on the blue wool scale; Decorative HPL reports 4 to 5 on the grey scale.

Why does real wood change colour?+−

Light degrades the wood surface. Researchers in Riga saw visible colour change in birch, oak, spruce and pine even under ordinary LED, incandescent and fluorescent lamps, and a 2026 study of 40 species found yellowing to be the dominant change under UV.

Is a wood-decor façade panel colour-stable?+−
