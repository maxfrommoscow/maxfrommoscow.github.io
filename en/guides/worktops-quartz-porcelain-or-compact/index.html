Quartz, porcelain or compact laminate worktops: the silica question for fabricators

Worktops are chosen for looks, heat and scratch resistance. For the people who cut them there is another question: crystalline silica. Spain's national occupational-safety institute, the Veneto region's health service and EU law show why quartz agglomerate needs particular care in the workshop — and where porcelain and compact laminate stand.

7 min read·Updated Oct 21, 2026

In short

  • Quartz agglomerate is 70–90 % crystalline silica, and cutting it releases respirable dust that EU law classes as carcinogenic, with a binding limit of 0.1 mg/m³.
  • Spain recorded 520 silicosis cases in 2024 alone, and Veneto more than 25 linked to artificial-stone workshops since 2016. The risk falls on the people who cut and fit the slabs, not on kitchen users.
  • Compact laminate contains no crystalline silica — it is cellulose with phenolic and melamine resins — so fabricators need only the dust extraction used for any panel. TOPAZ comes in 10–18 mm and formats up to 1830 × 4330 mm.

How this review was put together

This article draws on a technical note on machining quartz agglomerates by INSST, Spain's national institute for safety and health at work, and on the report on the resurgence of silicosis that INSST presented in April 2025; on a June 2023 alert from the health authorities of Italy's Veneto region; on Directive (EU) 2017/2398; on Cose di Casa's guide to porcelain worktops; and on Baunetz Wissen for the make-up of HPL. Each point is attributed, and the sources are listed at the end.

Three materials, three recipes

INSST's technical note describes quartz agglomerate as silica sand and quartz — sometimes with cristobalite — in grains below 4.5 mm, bound with glass, feldspars and colourants by polyester or acrylic resins. Its crystalline silica content ranges from 70 to 90 %, depending on colour and finish. It reached the market in the 1990s as an alternative to granite and marble, above all for kitchen and bathroom worktops, and is made in slabs of about 3 × 1.5 m, usually 12 to 30 mm thick.

Porcelain stoneware, Cose di Casa explains, is made from clays and quartz and feldspar sands with metal-oxide pigments, pressed and fired at over 1250 °C. For worktops, maxi slabs of 100 × 300 cm and more are usually used, only 3 to 3.5 mm thick and bonded to a flat, stable support.

Compact laminate is cellulose with phenolic and melamine resins, pressed under heat, according to Baunetz Wissen, and is machined with carbide-tipped woodworking tools. Crystalline silica is not among its ingredients.

Why crystalline silica matters

Directive (EU) 2017/2398 states that there is sufficient evidence of the carcinogenicity of respirable crystalline silica dust. It added work involving exposure to such dust generated by a work process to the EU carcinogens directive and set a binding limit of 0.1 mg/m³ for the respirable fraction; member states had to transpose it by 17 January 2020.

INSST explains the mechanism: whenever a material containing crystalline silica is crushed, cut, drilled, carved or ground, respirable silica particles can be released into the air of the workplace. The arrival of quartz agglomerates, with their high silica content, brought silicosis to trades where it had not occurred before, which is why the institute calls it an emerging risk.

What Spain reports

In April 2025 INSST and Spain's health ministry presented a study on the resurgence of silicosis. In 2024 alone, 520 cases of silicosis were reported to Spain's occupational disease register, and 5,900 between 2007 and 2024. The manufacture and handling of quartz agglomerates, together with the processing of natural stone — mainly granite and slate — account for the largest share of reports, 47.8 %. The study observed more cases among younger workers.

Among its recommendations: consider banning quartz agglomerates, or regulating work with them much more strictly, following the example of Australia and California.

What Veneto reports

The Veneto region's June 2023 alert counts more than 25 cases of silicosis, one of them fatal, attributable to exposure in artificial-stone companies since 2016, the latest diagnosed at the end of 2022. The youngest patient was just over 30, and in one case the disease appeared only two years after first exposure. In the province of Padua, 15 cases were diagnosed among the workers and former workers of a single company of about 110 people that made tops, sinks and fittings from marble, granite and quartz conglomerates.

The region found dry manual finishing with high-speed tools, without effective dust extraction, to be the riskiest operation.

What the workshop has to do

INSST's note covers both the workshop and installation on site, and sets out the measures in order of priority:

  • Substitute where possible. If materials without crystalline silica cannot be used instead, the aim is to minimise dust and keep it out of the air.
  • Reduce emission: machine wet, and use low-speed portable machines.
  • Capture at source: local exhaust ventilation where the dust is generated.
  • Clean equipment and work areas by wet or vacuum methods.
  • Then measure: exposure should be not merely below the limit but as low as technically possible. Respiratory protection is needed where these measures are not enough — and never replaces them.

Porcelain and laminate: different questions

Porcelain is not quartz agglomerate, but its recipe includes quartz sand, and INSST lists the ceramics industry among the sectors where crystalline silica exposure occurs. Ask the slab maker's safety data sheet for its crystalline silica content and control the dust accordingly.

Compact laminate raises a different question. Its ingredients contain no crystalline silica, so the silicosis risk the Spanish and Italian authorities describe does not come from it. Its dust should still be extracted, as with any panel cut in a workshop.

In the kitchen

In use the three differ mainly on heat and scratches. Cose di Casa credits porcelain with resistance to heat above 210 °C, to stains, impact and scratches. Compact laminate reaches the top grade for dry heat at 160 °C to EN 438-2, a laboratory condition rather than permission to put a pan straight from the hob on the surface; our review of HPL in Italian kitchens collects what the design press says about quartz and laminate in daily use.

Reading this against the Rushil range

TOPAZ, Rushil's compact worktop panel, is classified to EN 438-4 in standard and fire-retardant grades. Its data sheet gives 10–18 mm and formats up to 1830 × 4330 mm, and it has been tested for food contact. It is machined with woodworking tools; as with any panel, cut it with dust extraction.

Common questions

Is a quartz worktop dangerous in the kitchen?+−

The risk INSST and the Veneto health authorities describe is to the people who cut, grind and polish it — in the workshop and during installation — through respirable crystalline silica dust. These sources do not describe a risk from using a finished worktop.

What is the EU limit for crystalline silica dust?+−

0.1 mg/m³ for the respirable fraction, set by Directive (EU) 2017/2398, which member states had to transpose by 17 January 2020. INSST adds that exposure should be as low as technically possible, not merely below the limit.

Does cutting compact laminate expose workers to silica?+−

Crystalline silica is not among its ingredients — cellulose and phenolic and melamine resins, according to Baunetz Wissen. Machining still produces dust, so use extraction as with any panel.
