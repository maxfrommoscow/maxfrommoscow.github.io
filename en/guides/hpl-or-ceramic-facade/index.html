HPL or ceramic on a ventilated façade: what the European sources compare

Porcelain stoneware and HPL are both common claddings for ventilated façades, and each wins on different ground. Italian and German technical sources, a UK government advice note and the façade associations show where.

8 min read·Updated Oct 21, 2026

In short

  • HPL wins on weight and panel size: a 6 mm exterior compact panel weighs about 8.8 kg/m² and comes up to 1830 × 4330 mm, while one ceramic maker gives 30–40 kg/m² for its systems, substructure included.
  • It also wins on impact: Rushil's 6 mm exterior compact withstood an 1800 mm ball drop against a required 1400 mm, while ceramic slabs need a glass-fibre mesh on the back to hold them together if they break.
  • Ceramics win on fire: porcelain stoneware is A1, fire-retardant HPL reaches B-s1,d0 — so KLADS is not an option where A1 or A2 cladding is required.

How this review was put together

The question behind this article was whether HPL is pushing ceramics off façades. We found no published market data that would show it, so the article compares the two materials instead. It draws on the Italian engineering portal Ingenio and the building magazine Infobuild; on the German reference Baunetz Wissen; on the fire guideline of FVHF, the German association for ventilated façades; on Advice Note 22 of the Welsh Government; and on a leaflet of ICDLI, the international association of decorative laminate makers. Each point is attributed, and the sources are listed at the end.

What the market data says

Less than the question suggests. Ingenio's 2020 overview, which reproduces market data from UNICMI, the Italian building envelope association, noted that ventilated façades were still a smaller share of the building envelope market than curtain walls, and credited the spread of large ceramic slabs with their growth. Baunetz Wissen describes ventilated façades as among the most widespread façade systems today and reports that façade market studies show architects value above all their design possibilities, alongside functional safety.

Neither source, nor any open data we found, ranks HPL against ceramics; the paid market reports that compare cladding materials do not publish their figures. Infobuild's 2025 guide simply lists the options side by side: porcelain stoneware, appreciated for its resistance and range of finishes; metal sheets; and composite panels such as laminates or fibre cement, described as versatile and light. What the sources do document is where each material has the advantage.

Weight and the wall behind it

Infobuild warns that on existing buildings a ventilated façade needs a careful structural check, because its total weight can be high — especially with stone or large-format ceramic finishes. A ceramic manufacturer's technical note published by Infobuild puts its own porcelain systems at about 30–40 kg/m², substructure included.

Exterior compact HPL is lighter. Rushil's exterior compact has a density of 1.47 g/cm³, so the panel alone weighs about 8.8 kg/m² at 6 mm and 11.8 kg/m² at 8 mm. The figures are not like for like — the ceramic one includes rails and brackets — so compare complete systems; but on an old wall the difference can decide what the wall will carry.

Formats and joints

Baunetz Wissen gives the ceramic formats: glazed stoneware slabs up to 1.25 × 1.25 m as standard and, with special processes, up to 1.25 × 3.00 m; dry-pressed porcelain stoneware at 1.20 × 0.60 m as standard and up to 1.25 × 1.85 m as a special size; thicknesses of about 8–12 mm. Ingenio lists the formats most used on ventilated façades, from 30 × 30 cm to 120 × 278 cm, and notes that porcelain can easily be cut on site.

For HPL, Baunetz Wissen gives standard thicknesses of 6, 8 and 10 mm and panels of roughly 2.0 × 5.5 m, depending on the manufacturer. Rushil's exterior compact comes in formats up to 1830 × 4330 mm. Fewer, larger panels mean fewer joints and fixings per square metre — and a façade that reads as larger planes.

Movement: closer than it looks

Ingenio gives porcelain stoneware a linear thermal expansion of no more than 6.7 × 10⁻⁶ per kelvin. Rushil's exterior compact data sheet reports 6.81 × 10⁻⁶ — almost the same. The real difference is moisture: Baunetz Wissen notes that ceramic slabs change shape only with temperature, never with humidity, while HPL also moves as it takes up and gives off moisture.

Both still have to be fixed without restraint. Baunetz Wissen requires ceramic slabs and their rails to be mounted so that thermal movement does not build up stress, and never across the joint between two rails. For HPL the rule is one fixed point per panel and oversized holes everywhere else — set out in our guide to detailing HPL façades.

Breakage and impact

Ceramic is hard but brittle. The manufacturer's note published by Infobuild strongly recommends bonding a glass-fibre mesh of 3 × 3 or 4 × 4 mm to the back of each slab with a two-component polyurethane adhesive, so that the pieces stay together if a slab breaks until it is replaced — and makes it mandatory for façade areas next to pedestrian routes. Ingenio counts easy repair after vandalism and graffiti among ceramic's strengths.

Compact HPL does not shatter. In the EN 438-2 large-ball impact test, Rushil's 6 mm exterior compact withstood a drop height of 1800 mm, against a required 1400 mm, with an indentation smaller than 10 mm.

Fire: where ceramics hold their ground

Ingenio lists porcelain stoneware as non-combustible, class A1. HPL is combustible: the Welsh Government's Advice Note 22 explains that fire-retardant grades typically achieve class B-s1,d0, while panels without fire retardant can be class C or D, depending on thickness. For new residential buildings with a storey at 18 m or more, the 2019 note announced that Wales would limit external-wall materials to classes A1 and A2-s1,d0 — in line, it says, with many other EU member states — and added that it knew of no HPL panel meeting that classification.

Below that line the picture is different. The same note reports that a full-scale BS 8414 test of fire-retardant HPL (B-s1,d0) over stone wool met the BR 135 criteria, and that such systems can be safe when properly installed; any HPL over combustible insulation, by contrast, is very unlikely to resist fire spread. In Germany the FVHF guideline requires non-combustible insulation behind ventilated façades and fire barriers in the cavity for the larger building classes — barriers that are not needed when cladding, substructure, insulation and brackets are all non-combustible. ICDLI reports tests by the City of Vienna's testing authority in which fire-retardant HPL compact on a ventilated façade showed no vertical fire spread and no burning droplets; the tests were commissioned by the laminate industry.

The conclusion is plain: where national rules demand A1 or A2 cladding, ceramics are an answer and HPL is not. Elsewhere, fire-retardant HPL over mineral wool, tested as a system, is established practice.

Colour, surface and cleaning

Baunetz Wissen and Ingenio agree that ceramic does not fade under strong UV, resists stains and smog, and comes in wood, marble, stone and concrete effects. HPL relies on its exterior grade: Rushil's exterior compact was rated 4–5 on the grey scale after 3000 hours of artificial weathering to EN 438-2, against a required 3.

Reading this against the Rushil range

KLADS is Rushil's exterior compact to EN 438-6, documented from 6 to 12 mm and in formats up to 1830 × 4330 mm. Its fire-retardant EDF grade is classified B-s1,d0 at 6 mm. It is therefore not an option where A1 or A2 cladding is required, and it belongs over non-combustible insulation, as both the Welsh and the German sources expect. Where those rules allow it, its weight, panel size and impact resistance are the arguments for choosing it over ceramics.

Common questions

Is HPL replacing ceramics on façades?+−

We found no published data that shows it. Ingenio credited the growth of ventilated façades in Italy to large ceramic slabs. The two materials win on different criteria — HPL on weight, panel size and impact, ceramics on fire class.

Can HPL be used on a high-rise building?+−

It depends on national rules. Where A1 or A2 cladding is required — Wales for new residential buildings of 18 m or more, for example — the Welsh Government knows of no HPL that qualifies. Elsewhere, fire-retardant HPL over non-combustible insulation, tested as a system, is used.

Which is lighter, HPL or ceramic?+−

A 6 mm exterior compact panel weighs about 8.8 kg/m², from a density of 1.47 g/cm³. One ceramic manufacturer gives about 30–40 kg/m² for its systems, substructure included. Compare complete systems before deciding.
