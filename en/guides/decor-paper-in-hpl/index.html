Decor paper in HPL: why the pattern does not wear off

Every HPL surface starts as a sheet of paper: a solid colour, or a print of oak, concrete or marble. Yet a good laminate takes years of wiping, scrubbing and sliding cups without the pattern wearing through. The reason is what happens to that paper on the way into the press. Here is how the decor layer is built, how its wear and light resistance are measured, and what Rushil's surfaces achieve.

6 min read·Updated Nov 19, 2026

In short

  • The pattern of an HPL surface is pigmented or printed on a decor paper of 50 to 160 g/m², saturated with melamine resin. Printed decors also get a transparent overlay of 15 to 80 g/m² as an abrasion-resistant shield.
  • Pressed onto the kraft core under heat and high pressure, the melamine cures into a hard, non-porous surface with the decor sealed inside. Durability comes from the resin, the overlay and the press, not from the paper alone.
  • In the EN 438 wear test Rushil's compact reaches 450 revolutions and its Decorative HPL more than 400, where 150 are required, and TOPAZ holds 5 on the grey scale for light fastness, no visible change.

How this guide was put together

This guide draws on the processing guide of proHPL, the German HPL industry group, from January 2022; on the ICDLI's technical leaflet on the manufacture of HPL; on the wear test as described in ISO 4586-2, the international standard for decorative high-pressure laminates; and on Rushil's data sheets and the decor collection shown on this site.

What decor paper is

HPL is built from layers of paper saturated with resin and pressed under heat and high pressure. The core is kraft paper impregnated with phenolic resin. The face, in proHPL's words, is usually a decor paper impregnated with melamine resin. The ICDLI gives its weight as 50 to 160 g/m², pigmented in a solid colour or printed with wood, stone or any other design.

Melamine resin is the reason the face works. The ICDLI describes it as transparent, lightfast, hard and scratch-resistant, which is exactly what a surface needs and what paper alone is not. The decor paper carries the colour; the resin turns it into a working surface.

Solid colours and prints

A solid colour is pigmented through the paper, so the colour goes all the way down the decor layer. A print sits on the paper's surface, and that is why, as proHPL puts it, printed decor papers get an additional overlay: a transparent sheet of 15 to 80 g/m², also saturated with melamine, that forms an abrasion-resistant protective layer over the print.

Rushil uses that build where wear is highest. TOPAZ and VAULT compact carry a melamine-impregnated overlay over the decor paper, and the collection on this site offers 445 decors: 209 solid colours, 183 woods and 51 stones, in finishes from high gloss to matt.

Why the surface is harder than the paper

In the press the melamine melts, flows into the paper fibres and cures. The ICDLI describes the result as a homogeneous, rigid, fully cross-linked sheet that is non-porous and will not melt, with the fibres chemically bonded into it. The decor is not a coating on top of the laminate that could peel or flake; it is sealed inside the surface layer.

That answers a question buyers often ask: which decor paper is the strongest? The paper matters for colour and print quality, but wear resistance comes from the melamine, the overlay and the pressing. proHPL's summary is that HPL surfaces are hard and resistant to abrasion, impact and scratching, largely withstand vandalism, are easy to clean and last a long time.

How wear is measured

The international standard ISO 4586-2 describes the wear test like this. A specimen rotates against a pair of loaded wheels covered with abrasive paper and is checked every 25 revolutions. The initial wear point is the first clearly recognisable wear-through of the print, pattern or plain colour, and the result is counted in revolutions.

Measured to EN 438-2, Rushil's TOPAZ and VAULT compact reach 450 revolutions, three times the 150 that EN 438 requires, and the KLADS exterior compact reaches 450 too. Decorative HPL, the thin laminate for furniture and walls, exceeds 400 against more than 150 required. Scratch resistance on compact is grade 5, the top of the scale.

Colour that stays

Light fastness is tested to EN 438-2 against the grey scale, where 5 means no visible change. TOPAZ reaches 5 against a requirement of 4 to 5, and 6 on the blue wool scale against 4. Decorative HPL meets 4 to 5. The decor also resists staining: compact is graded 5, no change, in all three EN 438 staining groups.

In practice that means a printed oak keeps the colour of the sample it was chosen from. A comparison with real wood, which yellows even under indoor lamps, is set out in our guide to light fastness.

Choosing a decor for heavy use

Decor choice is mostly about looks, but a few points keep a good choice good.

  • For worktops, counters and other horizontal surfaces, specify the horizontal grade HGS or compact, not the vertical grade VGS.
  • Where prints take heavy wear, choose a build with an overlay; TOPAZ and VAULT have one as standard.
  • On Decorative HPL, textured finishes are rated 4 for scratching, against 3 for smooth ones; on compact, both reach 5.
  • Check the grain direction against the sheet format, and order a long run from one batch.
  • Ask for a current production sample, not an old chip, before you sign off a colour.

Common questions

Which decor papers are the most durable?+−

Durability does not come from the paper alone. The decor paper carries colour and print; wear resistance comes from the melamine resin it is saturated with, the overlay over printed decors and the high-pressure press. Compare laminates by their EN 438 wear result in revolutions.

Why does a printed HPL decor need an overlay?+−

Because the print sits on the paper's surface. A transparent overlay of 15 to 80 g/m², saturated with melamine, forms an abrasion-resistant layer over it. Solid colours are pigmented through the paper and can be built without one.

Will an HPL decor fade?+−

A good one hardly changes. Rushil's TOPAZ is graded 5 on the grey scale, no visible change, and 6 on the blue wool scale, against requirements of 4 to 5 and 4.
