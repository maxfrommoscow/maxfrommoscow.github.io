HPL façades in summer: what heat-flow research has measured

A ventilated façade is the building's sunshade. Researchers in Barcelona have measured and modelled one clad in HPL, and found that the colour of the panels, the air gap behind them and the wall beneath decide whether summer heat gets in.

6 min read·Updated Oct 22, 2026

In short

  • Researchers in Barcelona modelled a ventilated façade of 6 mm HPL and found that panel colour alone moved the daily heat flow between 1.33 W/m² into the building and 0.43 W/m² out of it.
  • The air gap matters too: a 6 cm cavity worked best with openings of about 4.5 cm, and the average heat flux fell as the cavity widened up to 10 cm.
  • KLADS keeps the designed colour — grey scale 4–5 after 3 000 hours of artificial weathering — and has a thermal conductivity of 0.2 W/(m·K).

How this review was put together

This article summarises three peer-reviewed papers on ventilated façades in summer: a study from the Universitat Politècnica de Catalunya in Barcelona of how colour and the air channel affect a façade's energy performance (Roig et al., Journal of Physics: Conference Series, 2023); a follow-up by the same team with colleagues in Italy comparing claddings, wall build-ups and cavities (Roig et al., Energy and Buildings, 2024); and an analytical method for summer performance from the University of Pisa (Ciampi, Leccese and Tuoni, Solar Energy, 2003). The Barcelona figures come from a calculation model checked against measurements on real buildings. The papers are listed at the end.

One façade clad in HPL

The Barcelona team validated its model on buildings it had monitored with temperature sensors through warm and cold periods. One of them is the Faculty of Chemistry at the Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona: a brick wall insulated on the outside and covered by a ventilated façade of 6 mm HPL panels. The model followed the measured temperatures closely, with coefficients of determination of 0.88 and above, most of them over 0.9, and errors of no more than 8.3 %.

With the model validated, the researchers changed one design choice at a time and calculated the daily average heat flow through the façade on a sunny summer day in Barcelona. The HPL façade is therefore not a laboratory sample but a real envelope, and the results below describe how its design choices play out.

Colour decides which way the heat flows

Colour came first. Varying how much sunlight the outer surface absorbs, from none to all, moved the daily average heat flow through the HPL façade between 1.33 W/m² into the building and 0.43 W/m² out of it. In other words, the choice of surface alone could turn the façade from adding heat to the rooms behind it into shedding heat from them. The authors note that the effect is not obvious by intuition: a darker skin heats up, but it also drives a stronger flow of air through the cavity, which carries heat away.

For an architect this makes colour a thermal decision as well as an aesthetic one, and HPL is a cladding that offers the whole range to choose from. The figures are for one sunny day in Barcelona; another climate and orientation need their own calculation.

The air gap and its openings

The second variable was the ventilation channel. For the HPL façade, changing the cavity and the openings at its top and bottom moved the daily heat flow between 0.90 and 0.37 W/m². The authors give a worked example: with a 6 cm cavity, openings of about 4.5 cm were the right size. Narrower openings held heat in the cavity; wider ones brought no further benefit.

The 2024 follow-up, which compared different claddings, wall build-ups and cavities, adds two findings. Lower cavities and open joints let in more fresh air and reduced net heat gains, and widening the cavity up to 10 cm lowered the average heat flux.

Stop the heat in the outer layers

The clearest message of the 2024 study is where to stop the sun's energy. In summer, the authors conclude, the best strategy against heat gains is to block energy in the outermost layers of the wall, which points to cladding materials that conduct heat poorly and to insulation on the outside of the main wall. Their abstract also notes that the façade with the lowest energy demand in use is not automatically the best over its whole life cycle, which they leave for further study.

HPL fits that recommendation. It is made of paper and resin rather than metal or stone, and Rushil's data sheet for the KLADS exterior compact gives a thermal conductivity of 0.2 W/(m·K) to EN 12524. On a façade insulated from the outside, as at the Barcelona faculty, the panel and the ventilated air gap form the first barrier the sun meets.

Design the façade as one system

The University of Pisa paper explains why these variables work together. Its analytical method describes the air rising through the cavity as the sun warms it and estimates the resulting saving in summer cooling. It identifies the width of the air duct, the position of the insulation, the solar radiation, the outer surface and even the roughness of the cavity's surfaces as the factors that matter most, and works through two typical cases: adding a ventilated façade to an existing masonry wall, and designing a new wall around a chosen outer facing. Both are cases where an exterior HPL panel is the visible layer of a system designed as a whole.

What this means for ventilated façades in KLADS

Read together, the studies give a clear brief. Choose the colour with its thermal effect in mind, because on the Barcelona HPL façade it decided whether heat came in or went out. Size the cavity and its openings in the same model. Keep the heat out at the outer layers, with insulation outside the wall and a cladding that conducts heat poorly, which is exactly what an HPL panel is.

KLADS is Rushil's exterior compact for ventilated façades, balconies and soffits. Its data sheet reports a thermal conductivity of 0.2 W/(m·K), colour held at grey-scale 4–5 with appearance grade 5 after 3,000 hours of artificial weathering and after 1,500 hours of UV exposure to EN 438-2, against required grades 3 and 4, and a dimensional change in heat of 0.10 % against a limit of 0.30 %. These are test results, not a declaration of conformity. The colour you model is the colour that stays on the building; samples and data sheets are available on request.

Common questions

Does the colour of an HPL façade affect how much heat enters the building?+−

Yes. On the HPL-clad Faculty of Chemistry in Barcelona, changing how much sunlight the cladding absorbs moved the calculated daily average heat flow on a sunny summer day between 1.33 W/m² into the building and 0.43 W/m² out of it. Colour is a thermal choice as well as an aesthetic one.

How wide should the cavity and its openings be?+−

It depends on the façade. In the Barcelona calculation for the HPL façade, a 6 cm cavity worked best with openings of about 4.5 cm, and the 2024 follow-up found lower average heat flux as the cavity widened up to 10 cm. Run the façade model with your building's own geometry and climate.
