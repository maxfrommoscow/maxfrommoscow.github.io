Formaldehyde from August 2026: the EU limit for furniture and wood-based articles

REACH entry 77 halves the value behind the familiar E1 class and makes it law in every member state. The Italian furniture laboratory CATAS, the trade press and the German environment agency explain what it changes for panel buyers and furniture makers.

8 min read·Updated Oct 20, 2026

In short

  • Since 6 August 2026, furniture and wood-based articles on the EU market may release no more than 0.062 mg/m³ of formaldehyde under the REACH entry 77 test conditions — half the 0.124 mg/m³ behind the E1 class.
  • The limit applies to the finished article. Conformity can be shown by checking the raw materials or by testing the product as sold, and a CARB or EPA certificate is not necessarily enough.
  • Rushil's data sheets report 0.010 mg/m³ for VAULT to EN 16516, and its emission summary reports E0 to EN 16516 — test results, not a declaration of conformity with entry 77.

How this review was put together

This article draws on the text of Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/1464; on three Italian trade publications — Xylon, Ambiente Cucina and Furnishing Idea — that reported the rule through interviews with the furniture testing laboratory CATAS and the furniture federation FederlegnoArredo; on the German Federal Environment Agency (Umweltbundesamt); on CATAS's own guidance from June 2026; and on Certifico's summary of the European Chemicals Agency's measurement guideline. Each point is attributed, and the sources are listed at the end.

What the rule says

Regulation (EU) 2023/1464 added entry 77 to Annex XVII of REACH. Since 6 August 2026, articles may not be placed on the EU market if, under the test conditions of its Appendix 14, the formaldehyde they release exceeds 0.062 mg/m³ for furniture and wood-based articles, or 0.080 mg/m³ for other articles. Road vehicles follow a year later: from 6 August 2027 the concentration inside a vehicle may not exceed 0.062 mg/m³.

The recitals explain the split. Wood-based panels, articles made from them and furniture are the main sources of formaldehyde in indoor air, particularly in newly built homes, so they get the lower value — applied, in the Regulation's words, to the whole complex product. The Commission gave industry 36 months, longer than the 24 months ECHA's Committee for Socio-economic Analysis had recommended for most sectors.

The German Federal Environment Agency confirms that the European rule has applied since 6 August 2026 and that the formaldehyde provisions of Germany's Chemicals Prohibition Ordinance have been obsolete since then. In July 2026 it cut its long FAQ on the subject down to that single answer.

Half of E1 — and law everywhere

In an interview with Xylon in November 2023, CATAS director Franco Bulian traced the history. In 1989 the World Health Organization indicated that indoor concentrations should stay below 0.124 mg/m³; Germany acted first, and the work on test methods led to the E1 class for wood-based products. Italy made the E1 panel mandatory only in 2008, and other European countries did the same — but not all of them. The new limit is exactly half the old value.

Furnishing Idea and Ambiente Cucina put the change in the same terms: 0.062 mg/m³ is 50 % of E1, and unlike E1 it must be applied by every EU country, imports included. Bulian added two points that matter to buyers. For particleboard and MDF the new limit is lower even than the strict US rules, so a board that meets the CARB or EPA limits cannot automatically be used in Europe. And in Europe it is the manufacturer who declares that a product complies; there is no obligation to certify, although having production checked by a recognised laboratory is, in his words, widespread responsible practice.

Where the formaldehyde comes from

Bulian explained the mechanism to Xylon. Wood-based panels are made of particles, veneers or fibres held together by a binder, usually a urea adhesive containing formaldehyde. Moisture degrades the adhesive, formaldehyde forms inside the panel and, being a gas, escapes from the panel and from the furniture made with it — all the more in warm, humid rooms.

He also stressed that formaldehyde is everywhere, wood included: the best achievable panel would contain no more than the wood it is made from. The Regulation reflects this — articles in which formaldehyde is present only naturally, from the materials they are made of, are exempt. And because buildings are ever more airtight, whatever furniture and building materials release is less easily dispersed.

How compliance is measured

Appendix 14 fixes the reference conditions: a test chamber at 23 ± 0.5 °C and 45 ± 3 % relative humidity, a loading of 1 ± 0.02 m² of test piece per m³ of chamber, and one air change per hour. The concentration is measured at least twice a day, at least three hours apart, until a steady state is reached; the test may not exceed 28 days, and the steady-state value is compared with the limit. The loading factor corresponds to wood-based panels; for other products the loading factors of EN 16516 may be used where 1 m²/m³ is clearly unrealistic.

The original proposal named EN 717-1 as the method; the final text replaced it with a description of conditions so that other methods and other articles are covered. Results from non-reference conditions may be used where there is a scientifically valid correlation with the reference conditions.

At the Commission's request ECHA published a measurement guideline in 2025. According to Certifico's summary, it covers chamber methods (EN 717-1, EN 16516 and the EN ISO 16000 series), derived methods for wood-based panels, how to test furniture and other articles, and the principles of correlating data; questions of scope are left to a separate Q&A. Bulian had anticipated this in Furnishing Idea: the guidelines would let laboratories use derived methods and simplify the checks companies need.

The exemptions that matter on a project

Entry 77 lists ten exemptions. For furniture makers and specifiers the relevant ones are these:

  • Articles exclusively for outdoor use under foreseeable conditions.
  • Articles in constructions that are used only outside the building shell and vapour barrier and do not emit formaldehyde into indoor air.
  • Articles exclusively for industrial or professional use — unless their emissions expose the general public under foreseeable conditions of use.
  • Articles intended to come into contact, directly or indirectly, with food within the scope of Regulation (EC) No 1935/2004.
  • Second-hand articles, and articles in which formaldehyde is present only naturally.

What CATAS tells manufacturers to check

In its "last call" of 30 June 2026, CATAS listed what companies in the wood and furniture chain should verify: the emission characteristics of the raw materials and components they buy; process changes that can affect formaldehyde release; the emission performance of the product actually placed on the market; the technical and documentary evidence of conformity; and the correct application of the test methods. Conformity, it noted, can be assessed either by checking the individual raw materials or by testing the product in the configuration in which it is sold.

Omar Degoli of FederlegnoArredo told Ambiente Cucina in 2023 that compliant solutions were already available for most products, but that companies should plan the time needed to adapt and to rotate their stock, and he hoped for equal rigour in market surveillance so that imported products and semi-finished goods also meet the limit.

Reading this against the Rushil range

Rushil's data sheets report formaldehyde results under their own methods: the VAULT data sheet gives 0.010 mg/m³ to EN 16516, the TOPAZ data sheet gives class E1 to EN 717-2, and Rushil's food-contact and emission summary reports E0 to EN 16516 with ISO 16000-3. These are test results, not a declaration of conformity with entry 77, whose limit applies to the article placed on the market under the Appendix 14 conditions or a correlated method.

For a furniture maker the article is the finished piece: board, adhesive, edging and laminate together. Ask each supplier for a test report that states the method, the loading factor and the conditioning, and keep it with the product file — that is the evidence CATAS describes.

Common questions

Does the 0.062 mg/m³ limit apply to a panel or to the finished furniture?+−

To furniture and wood-based articles; the Regulation's recitals say the value applies to the whole complex product. CATAS notes that conformity can be shown by checking the raw materials or by testing the product as sold.

Is E1 still enough?+−

Not as an EU benchmark. E1 rests on 0.124 mg/m³; since 6 August 2026 furniture and wood-based articles must stay at or below 0.062 mg/m³ under the Appendix 14 conditions or a correlated method.

Are façade panels covered?+−

Articles used exclusively outdoors, and articles in constructions used only outside the building shell and vapour barrier that do not emit into indoor air, are exempt. Interior linings and furniture are not.

Is a CARB or EPA certificate enough for Europe?+−

Not necessarily. CATAS's director told Xylon that for particleboard and MDF the EU limit is lower than the US rules, so a board meeting CARB or EPA limits may not be usable in Europe.
