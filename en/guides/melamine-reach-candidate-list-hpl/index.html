Melamine on the REACH Candidate List: what it means for HPL

The melamine resin that gives HPL its hard, cleanable surface is made from a substance the EU now lists as being of very high concern. Buyers ask what that means. In short, the duties depend on how much melamine as such is in a product, and Rushil's TOPAZ screening puts every listed substance at or below the threshold.

6 min read·Updated Oct 23, 2026

In short

  • Melamine has been on the REACH Candidate List since January 2023. That triggers information duties only where an article contains more than 0.1 % by weight — it is not a ban.
  • ECHA has recommended melamine for authorisation, but notes that its use as an intermediate in making resins — up to 95 % of the melamine in the EU — appears to fall outside it.
  • Rushil's TOPAZ screening puts every one of the 253 Candidate List substances at or below 0.1 %, and no melamine migrated into food simulant.

How this guide was put together

This guide is based on ECHA's Candidate List and its summary of the obligations that follow from it; on ECHA's November 2025 recommendation for melamine and the background document behind it; on a question-and-answer paper from EMPA, the European melamine producers' association; and on Rushil's TOPAZ data sheet. It explains EU chemicals law as it applies to laminates; it is not legal advice.

Why melamine is in every HPL

Melamine resin is made from melamine and formaldehyde, and it is what gives a decorative laminate its surface. EMPA notes that it adds heat, water and chemical resistance, and laminate makers impregnate the decor and overlay papers with it before pressing. In the press the resin cures into a cross-linked network.

ECHA's background document describes melamine's main use as a monomer for melamine resins, in which melamine as such is present only as residual monomer. That distinction, between melamine reacted into a resin and melamine left over as a free substance, is the one that matters for the rules below.

What the listing says

ECHA added melamine to the Candidate List of substances of very high concern on 17 January 2023, under Article 57(f) of REACH, for an equivalent level of concern to human health and the environment. In its recommendation ECHA describes that concern as a combination of very high persistence, high mobility in water, the potential to travel long distances in water, and toxicity.

The list now has 253 entries. It was last updated on 4 February 2026 and, as no new proposals were submitted during 2026, ECHA expects the next update in June 2027.

Who has to do what

Inclusion triggers duties along the supply chain. EU and EEA suppliers of articles that contain a Candidate List substance above 0.1 % by weight must give their customers enough information to use the article safely, and must answer a consumer's request within 45 days. Producers and importers must notify ECHA where the substance is present above 0.1 % and in more than one tonne a year, and suppliers must report such articles to ECHA's SCIP database under the Waste Framework Directive.

Every one of these duties turns on the 0.1 % threshold. For a laminate, the question is therefore how much melamine as such the finished sheet contains, and that is answered by testing.

Authorisation: what is proposed, and what is not

ECHA went a step further in its 12th recommendation, finalised on 18 November 2025, and recommended melamine for the Authorisation List, Annex XIV of REACH, with a latest application date 24 months after inclusion and a sunset date 18 months after that. The European Commission decides, and melamine is not on the Authorisation List today.

ECHA's background document notes that use as an intermediate in the production of resins and polymers appears to fall outside the scope of authorisation, and industry put intermediate uses at up to 95 % of the melamine manufactured or imported in the EU. EMPA adds that placing an article containing an Annex XIV substance on the market does not itself require authorisation. Whether a given use counts as an intermediate is for each company to assess, ECHA stresses.

What Rushil's tests show

Rushil's TOPAZ data sheet reports a screening against all 253 substances on ECHA's Candidate List, the same list in force today, with every tested substance at or below 0.1 % by weight; the full test report is available on request. The same data sheet reports that specific migration of melamine into food simulant was not detected, against a limit of 2.5 mg/kg. These are test results, not a declaration of conformity.

For a buyer that is the useful answer: no Candidate List substance above the threshold that triggers information duties, and no detectable melamine in the migration test.

What to ask a laminate supplier

Ask for an SVHC screening against the current Candidate List, dated after its last update; ask whether any listed substance exceeds 0.1 % by weight, and if so which; and, for food-contact uses, ask for migration results. For TOPAZ the answers are on the data sheet, and samples and the full reports are available on request.

Common questions

Is melamine banned in the EU?+−

No. Melamine is on the REACH Candidate List of substances of very high concern, which triggers information duties above 0.1 % by weight in articles. ECHA has recommended it for the Authorisation List, but the European Commission has not decided, and ECHA notes that use as an intermediate in making resins appears to fall outside authorisation.

Does HPL contain more than 0.1 % melamine?+−

It depends on the product and has to be tested. Rushil's TOPAZ data sheet reports every one of the 253 Candidate List substances at or below 0.1 % by weight, and no detectable melamine in the food-contact migration test.

Who has to tell customers about an SVHC in a laminate?+−

EU and EEA suppliers of articles that contain a Candidate List substance above 0.1 % by weight. They must also answer consumer requests within 45 days and report such articles to ECHA's SCIP database.
