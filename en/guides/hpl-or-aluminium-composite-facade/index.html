HPL or aluminium composite on a façade: what the fire rules say after Grenfell

Aluminium composite panels and HPL compete for the same ventilated façades, and both come in grades that behave very differently in a fire. The official record — the Grenfell Tower Inquiry, the UK government's tests, England's regulations and German building law — shows what separates them.

8 min read·Updated Oct 21, 2026

In short

  • The Grenfell Tower Inquiry found that aluminium composite panels with polyethylene cores were the principal reason the fire spread so fast; fire-retardant HPL over stone wool met the BR 135 criteria in a UK government test.
  • The Welsh Government judged the risk from unsafe HPL systems lower than that from category 3 aluminium composite, but knows of no HPL that reaches A2-s1,d0.
  • Where England (from 18 m) or German high-rise rules require A1 or A2 cladding, HPL — KLADS included, classified B-s1,d0 — cannot be used, and an A2-cored aluminium composite can.

How this review was put together

This article draws on the executive summary of the Grenfell Tower Inquiry's Phase 1 report; on the seven large-scale fire tests of aluminium composite cladding that the UK government published in 2017; on England's building regulations of 2018 and 2022 and the government's explanatory memorandum; on Advice Note 22 of the Welsh Government on HPL; on an answer of the Bavarian state government to its parliament on façade fire rules; and on Baunetz Wissen. Each point is attributed, and the sources are listed at the end.

Two panels, several grades

An aluminium composite panel (ACM), as Baunetz Wissen describes it, is two aluminium cover sheets bonded to a plastic core whose material and thickness depend on the fire performance required. Baunetz lists cores with mineral additives for the German classes B1 and A2, and cores of polyethylene, which put a panel in class B2. The UK government's 2017 tests covered the same three families: unmodified polyethylene, fire-retardant polyethylene and a limited-combustibility filler.

HPL has no metal skin and no separate core: Baunetz Wissen describes it as cellulose with phenolic and melamine resins, pressed under heat. Its grades differ in fire retardant. The Welsh Government's Advice Note 22 says that fire-retardant grades typically achieve class B-s1,d0, that panels without fire retardant can be class C or D depending on thickness, and that it knows of no HPL panel reaching A2-s1,d0. An A2 grade, in other words, exists for aluminium composite but not for HPL.

What Grenfell showed

The Grenfell Tower Inquiry's Phase 1 report, published in October 2019, found that the principal reason the flames spread so rapidly up, down and around the tower was the presence of aluminium composite rainscreen panels with polyethylene cores, which acted as a source of fuel; melting and dripping polyethylene carried the fire sideways and downwards. Polyisocyanurate (PIR) and phenolic foam insulation behind the panels contributed to the rate and extent of vertical spread.

The chairman did not need to recommend that polyethylene-cored panels on high-rise buildings be replaced with materials of limited combustibility, he wrote, because it was already accepted that this must be done — and as quickly as possible.

What the government's tests showed

In July and August 2017 the UK government published seven large-scale tests of aluminium composite cladding systems to BS 8414, each judged against the criteria of its guidance BR 135. Both systems with an unmodified polyethylene core failed, one over PIR foam and one over stone wool. Fire-retardant polyethylene cores failed over PIR and over phenolic foam and met the criteria over stone wool. Panels with a limited-combustibility filler met the criteria over both PIR foam and stone wool.

HPL was tested later. Advice Note 22 reports a government test of fire-retardant HPL (B-s1,d0) over stone wool that met the BR 135 criteria — a combination chosen because industry said it was common on residential buildings of 18 m or more. The Welsh expert panel had seen no evidence of HPL of any class meeting the criteria over combustible insulation and judged systems with class C or D panels very unlikely to resist fire spread. It also judged the risk from unsafe HPL systems to be lower than that from unsafe systems with category 3 aluminium composite panels, whose removal it called the absolute priority.

The lesson of both series is that panel and insulation are judged together: the same fire-retardant aluminium composite passed over stone wool and failed over foam.

England's rules since 2018

The Building (Amendment) Regulations 2018, in force since 21 December 2018, require materials that become part of the external wall of a 'relevant building' in England to be class A2-s1,d0 or A1, with listed exceptions such as membranes, seals and fixings, and window frames and glass. A relevant building has a storey at least 18 m above ground and contains dwellings, an institution or rooms for residential purposes.

The Building etc. (Amendment) (England) Regulations 2022, in force since 1 December 2022, went further. They bar 'relevant metal composite material' from the external walls of any building, whatever its height: panels no more than 10 mm thick with two or more metal layers and a substantial layer whose gross calorific value exceeds 35 MJ/kg. The government's explanatory memorandum says this implements its proposal to ban metal composite panels with an unmodified polyethylene core, the type used on Grenfell Tower. The same regulations brought hotels, hostels and boarding houses within the 18 m rule.

HPL is not a metal composite, so the 2022 ban does not apply to it; but as no HPL reaches A2-s1,d0, it cannot go into the external wall of a relevant building. Wales sets its own rules: the 2019 Welsh note announced the same A1-or-A2 limit for new residential buildings of 18 m or more.

Germany: the line is drawn at 22 metres

In Germany each state sets its own building code. Answering a question in the Bavarian parliament in 2017, the state government summarised its rules for external wall cladding and insulation: normally flammable materials are allowed in building classes 1 to 3; in classes 4 and 5 below the high-rise threshold of 22 m they must be at least 'schwerentflammbar' — flame-retardant; on high-rise buildings they must be non-combustible.

These are national terms, not Euroclasses. Table 1.2 of the model administrative regulation on technical building rules (MVV TB) maps one onto the other; Baunetz Wissen notes that only non-combustible materials can be classed A1 or A2, and that under the current table a product not classified at least s2 for smoke is no longer 'schwerentflammbar', whatever its class. Baunetz gives polyethylene-cored aluminium composite the national class B2 — normally flammable — which, on the Bavarian summary, confines it to building classes 1 to 3.

Where that leaves the two materials

Read together, the sources draw three lines. Polyethylene-cored aluminium composite is barred from new work on any building in England and, in Germany, limited to the lowest building classes. Where a code demands non-combustible or A2 cladding — England's relevant buildings of 18 m or more, Germany's high-rise buildings — an A2-cored aluminium composite can qualify and HPL cannot. Between those lines both fire-retardant panels are used, and the tests say the same about both: over stone wool they met the criteria; over foam insulation they failed or are not expected to pass.

The tests also show why a panel's class is not the whole answer. BR 135 judges a complete system, and the Welsh note stresses that safety depends on the installation, extent and arrangement of the system as well as on its materials.

Reading this against the Rushil range

KLADS, Rushil's exterior compact to EN 438-6, is HPL. Its fire-retardant EDF grade is classified B-s1,d0 at 6 mm, so it cannot be used where A1 or A2 cladding is required. Where it is allowed, the evidence above points to the fire-retardant grade over non-combustible insulation, with the system's classification or test evidence checked for the building in question.

Common questions

Is HPL banned in England?+−

Not outright. The 2022 ban on metal composite panels does not cover HPL, but since December 2018 materials in the external walls of residential and similar buildings with a storey at 18 m or more must be A2-s1,d0 or A1 — and the Welsh Government knows of no HPL that reaches A2.

Is fire-retardant aluminium composite safer than fire-retardant HPL?+−

The official tests do not rank them. Each met the BR 135 criteria over stone wool. Fire-retardant aluminium composite failed over PIR and phenolic foam, and the Welsh expert panel saw no evidence of HPL of any class passing over combustible insulation.

Which aluminium composite can go on a German high-rise building?+−

German high-rise buildings, from 22 m, need non-combustible external wall cladding, according to the Bavarian state government's summary. Only non-combustible materials can be classed A1 or A2, so only an A2-cored panel is a candidate — subject to its own approval and the state's rules.
