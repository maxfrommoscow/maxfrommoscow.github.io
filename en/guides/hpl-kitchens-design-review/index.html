HPL in the kitchen: what the Italian design press and EuroCucina 2026 show

Italian kitchen makers use HPL for far more than a worktop on a chipboard core. The design press shows where the material is heading; the trade press shows what a thin top asks of the planner.

7 min read·Updated Oct 20, 2026

In short

  • Italian kitchen makers use HPL far beyond a worktop on chipboard: 12 mm compact tops with integrated basins, sinks in the same decor, and stone, concrete and fluted-wood effects at EuroCucina 2026.
  • Designmag's 2026 comparison credits HPL with convincing stone and wood looks, a warmer feel, lower weight and precise cutting on site — and, in some cases, a saving of more than half against a quartz top.
  • Thin tops change how sinks and hobs are mounted, so plan both together. Rushil's TOPAZ is documented from 10 mm, in formats up to 1830 × 4330 mm, and tested for food contact.

How this review was put together

This article draws on the Italian home magazine Cose di Casa, which has covered kitchen worktop materials, integrated sinks and EuroCucina 2026 in detail; on the Salone del Mobile's own editorial coverage of EuroCucina 2026; on the Italian design site Designmag; and on the German trade magazine Küchenplaner for the installation side. Each point is attributed, and the sources are listed at the end.

The kitchens are named by their makers, because they are the projects. The laminate brands behind some of them are not: other manufacturers' products are not the subject of this site, and the solutions apply to any compact laminate that meets the same requirements.

Two different products called laminate

Cose di Casa's 2025 worktop guide describes the most common laminate top as a board core — particleboard or MDF — under a decorative laminate face, with HPL as the best grade. It notes that HPL can be post-formed around a curved edge, that laminate tops need a metal edge profile, and that the joints to the wall and the edges must be sealed to keep water out of the core.

The thin tops in the same magazine are a different product: self-supporting compact panels with no board inside. One Cose di Casa example is Scavolini's LiberaMente, where a matt red laminate covers the doors and forms a 12 mm worktop with integrated basins. Our guide to specifying a compact worktop covers that product in technical terms; this article is about what designers are doing with it.

The thin top, and what it asks of the planner

Thinness is the design attraction, and it is also an installation question. In 2017 Küchenplaner reported on a German kitchen maker's laminate worktop built from an extra-strong 1.2 mm laminate over a 9.4 mm functional layer, which the maker said was robust enough to take flush-mounted sinks and hobs.

An earlier Küchenplaner survey of slim worktops — written about glass, stone and steel, though the mechanics are the same for any thin top — warned that standard sink clamps are designed for worktops 30 to 40 mm thick, so thinner tops need replacement clamps, adapters or underpinning. Undermount and flush sinks are normally bonded with special two-component adhesives rather than screwed; one sink maker said its stainless steel sinks with integrated drainers need at least 30 mm of worktop or underpinning; and a dishwasher under the drainer can leave too little room for the mounting brackets on a very thin top.

For a compact top of 10–12 mm, the lesson is to choose the sink, the hob and their mounting method together with the worktop, not after it.

Sink and worktop in one material

Cose di Casa's October 2026 survey of integrated sinks presents them as one of the clearest uses of a continuous surface: easier to clean and visually calmer, especially on islands and peninsulas. Its HPL examples include CREO Kitchens' Integra, where the island sink is made in the same black-core, marble-effect HPL as the worktop, so there is no break in colour or finish.

In Ar-Tre's Up Design, an HPL peninsula top coordinated with the backsplash takes a large black sink with a drainer; in Arredo3's Kalì, the sink is integrated into a blue laminate top whose colour is matched by the base units. In each case the worktop, the bowl and often the wall behind read as one material.

Stone effects, without the stone

The Salone del Mobile's coverage of EuroCucina 2026 reports that marble-effect materials — porcelain stoneware, marbled composite quartz and marbled laminates — remain among the most popular in kitchen design because they combine a refined look with resistance to stains, scratches and heat. Among its examples, Stosa's Infinity pairs a worktop and back panel in an HPL decor called Black Gold.

Cose di Casa's report from the same fair describes it in terms of continuity: surfaces that run without interruption, veining that crosses doors and worktops, and largely monolithic volumes. Its laminate examples include a Stosa composition built around a travertine-effect HPL surface, and Lago's 36e8 Formae, whose base and tall-unit fronts are in structured laminate with concrete and fluted-wood finishes.

Designmag's 2026 comparison of quartz and HPL explains the appeal on cost and handling: HPL convincingly reproduces wood, natural stone and raw concrete, feels warmer to the touch than mineral surfaces, is lighter, can be cut precisely on site during fitting, and can save more than half the cost of a quartz top. Quartz, in its assessment, wins on scratch resistance.

What the press warns about

The sources agree on three points. Heat: Cose di Casa rates laminate's heat resistance as moderate and advises against putting hot pans on it, and Designmag says both quartz and HPL need protecting from heat. Joints: Designmag singles out the seams between pieces as the place where moisture can get in, and Cose di Casa stresses sealing at the wall and at the edges. Cleaning: a damp sponge and neutral detergent, with no abrasives, scouring powders or descalers, according to Cose di Casa.

These match the test data in our own guides: the compact grades reach the top grade for dry heat at 160 °C to EN 438-2.16, which is a laboratory condition, not permission to put a pan straight from the hob on the surface.

Reading this against the Rushil range

Rushil's compact worktop panel, TOPAZ, is classified to EN 438-4 in standard and fire-retardant grades. Its data sheet gives 10–18 mm (the Compact Verse catalogue says 10–22 mm) and formats up to 1830 × 4330 mm, and it has been tested for food contact and for odour and taste transfer. Interior compact grades have a black phenolic core, so a dark edge line — as in CREO Kitchens' Integra — is part of the look; a core coloured to match the face is not documented for the Rushil range on this site.

Long panels reduce the number of joints, which the press identifies as the weak point. For designs that use a bonded laminate with a curved edge, Rushil's Solid & Unicolor catalogue lists post-forming availability decor by decor. Integrated sinks are fabrication work — Rushil supplies the panel, the kitchen fabricator makes the bowl — so confirm the detail and its warranty with the fabricator.

Common questions

How thin can an HPL worktop be?+−

The Italian press shows compact laminate tops of 12 mm with integrated basins, and Rushil's TOPAZ is documented from 10 mm. Thin tops change how sinks and hobs are mounted, so plan the mounting method together with the worktop.

Can the sink be made from the same HPL as the worktop?+−

Several Italian kitchens shown by Cose di Casa do exactly that, with the bowl in the same decor and finish as the top. It is specialist fabrication: confirm the method and the warranty with the kitchen fabricator.

Is an HPL worktop cheaper than quartz?+−

Designmag's 2026 comparison puts the saving at more than half in some cases, with quartz ahead on scratch resistance. This site publishes no prices; request a quotation for the panel supply.
