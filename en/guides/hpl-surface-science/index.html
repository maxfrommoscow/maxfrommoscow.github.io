The HPL surface under the microscope: cracks, stains and fingerprints

The melamine surface is the part of HPL everyone touches. Materials scientists have studied how it forms, when it cracks and how it can be made to shed stains and fingerprints. This is what they found.

7 min read·Updated Oct 22, 2026

In short

  • HPL is 50–70 % paper and 30–50 % thermosetting resin, and its hard melamine surface is made in the press: how well the resin cures decides its resistance to scratches, stains and heat.
  • The cracking studies concern thin laminates glued to boards that move differently; compact is not glued to a board, so that mismatch does not arise.
  • Rushil's food-contact tests found no detectable melamine in three successive migrations, and its compact data sheets report grade 5 for staining, scratching and crazing.

How this review was put together

This article summarises six peer-reviewed papers: a study of cracking from the Fraunhofer Institute for Wood Research (Lukowsky and Meder, Applied Sciences, 2022); two on how melamine resin cures, from the Wood K plus research centre and Reutlingen University (Weiss et al., Polymers, 2020) and from the University of Porto (Santos et al., Proceedings of the IMechE, Part C, 2021); one from the German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment on how cure chemistry affects what melamine resin releases (Ebner et al., Molecules, 2020); and two on stains and fingerprints (Badila et al., Journal of Applied Polymer Science, 2014; Chen et al., RSC Advances, 2019). Most of the work is on thin laminates and laboratory samples; the text says where.

Paper, resin and moisture

HPL is 50 to 70 % paper and 30 to 50 % thermosetting resin by weight, the Fraunhofer authors note, and the paper moves with moisture. The literature they review gives a thermal expansion of 14 to 17 × 10⁻⁶/K for HPL, and tensile strengths for four HPLs of 76 N/mm² at 10 % relative humidity, 84 at 65 % and 66 at 90 %. Laminates shrink and swell with moisture almost exactly as much as particleboard does; gypsum boards hardly move at all.

When does a laminate crack?

EN 438-2 tests resistance to cracking by clamping a laminate sample and drying it at 50 °C for six hours, so that its shrinkage is restrained. The Fraunhofer team tested 43 thin laminates this way and then extended the test until moisture equilibrium. The composition mattered most: laminates with a backing of plain paper, which on its own shrank by 1.5 %, about four times as much as the other laminates, were the most prone to crack. In the deliberately prolonged test, 21 of the 43 eventually failed.

Real life is gentler. Even the laminate that failed the standard test did not crack after 14 days at 25 % and 10 % relative humidity; cracks appeared only at 50 °C. In furniture-door-size tests, the critical combination was a laminate of low cracking resistance, a mineral board that does not move with moisture, a laminate glued while damp, and PVAc glue. And cracks tend to start at sharp cut-outs. Compact HPL, which is not glued to a board, avoids the board mismatch altogether.

A network built in five steps

The surface's hardness comes from how the melamine resin cures. Using real-time infrared spectroscopy, researchers in Austria and Reutlingen divided the cure into five phases: a stationary phase with free methylol groups; a flexible network cross-linked by ether bridges; a rigid ether-linked network; further hardening as methylols and ethers turn into methylene cross-links; and a final consolidation of ether into methylene bridges. In Porto, the degree to which the resin had already condensed before impregnation affected its pot life, its reaction temperatures, the cross-linking reached and, in the end, the surface quality of the HPL.

Why the cure matters beyond looks

The German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment studied melamine kitchen utensils at 100 °C in 3 % acetic acid, conditions far harsher than a worktop sees, and found that how much melamine was released depended on the resin's chemistry: the ratio of methylene bridges to dimethylene ether bridges formed during curing. The study concerns moulded kitchenware, not laminates, but it shows why the late phases of the cure, in which ethers turn into methylene bridges, are worth completing.

Rushil's own food-contact tests, explained in a separate article on this site, found no detectable melamine: in SGS tests to EN 13130-1 it was not detected in any of three successive migrations (reporting limit 0.5 mg/kg, legal limit 2.5). Formaldehyde measured 5.31 and 4.29 mg/kg in the first two migrations and was not detected in the third, against a limit of 15.

Stains and fingerprints

Standard melamine surfaces can be improved further. In Qingdao, researchers modified the melamine resin of HPL with polyvinyl alcohol and benzoguanamine. After 24 hours under a drop of hot coffee or ink, the standard laminate kept stains in their test, while the modified one showed no obvious change; it also burned less readily in limiting-oxygen-index and cone calorimeter tests.

In Austria, a silicone-based additive in a low-pressure melamine film for furniture fronts raised the contact angle of water from 79.8° to 108.5°, and of squalene, an oily component of skin grease, from 31.4° to 59.3°: the surface shed water and fingerprints more readily and cleaned more easily, while its hardness, roughness and gloss stayed within requirements.

What this means for a specification

The research supports four practical points. Ask for cracking resistance on thin laminates, and do not glue damp laminate onto mineral boards with PVAc glue. Round the inside corners of cut-outs. Look at EN 438 surface grades, which reflect how well the resin has cured; Rushil's compact data sheets report grade 5, the top of the scale, for staining, scratching and crazing. And treat easy-clean additives as a developing field: they work in the laboratory, but each one has to keep the other surface properties intact.

Common questions

Why do some laminates crack?+−

Because the paper in them shrinks as it dries, and the shrinkage is restrained by the board or a clamp. In a Fraunhofer study, laminates with a plain paper backing were the most prone to crack, and the critical real-life combination was a low-resistance laminate glued damp onto a mineral board with PVAc glue.

Does compact HPL crack like thin laminate?+−

The cracking studies concern thin laminates glued to boards, where the board and laminate move differently. Compact is not glued to a board, so that mismatch does not arise; it still moves with moisture, which is why fixings allow for movement and inside corners are rounded.

Can HPL surfaces be made to resist fingerprints?+−

In research, yes. A silicone-based additive in a melamine film raised the contact angle of water from 79.8° to 108.5° and of skin-oil component squalene from 31.4° to 59.3°, improving cleanability without changing hardness, roughness or gloss beyond requirements.
