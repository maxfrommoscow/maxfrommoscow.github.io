Reading a fire classification: what B-s1, d0 actually tells you

A fire classification belongs to a grade at a tested thickness, not to a product name. Most specification errors in this area come from forgetting that.

7 min read·Updated Aug 13, 2026

In short

  • B-s1, d0 is three results in one: B for the contribution to a fire, s1 for the lowest smoke class, d0 for no flaming droplets. B is the best class a laminate can reach.
  • Rushil's fire-retardant compact clears Class B with a wide margin in the SBI test: a FIGRA of 26.1 W/s against a 120 W/s limit and total heat release of 2.4 MJ against 7.5 MJ.
  • The classification belongs to the fire-retardant grade at the tested thickness, not to the product name, and a panel class does not certify a façade system.

Three measurements, not one rating

EN 13501-1 does not give a product one score. It gives three, and they are reported together as something like B-s1, d0.

The letter is the main class, measuring how much the material contributes to a fire. A to F, where A1 is non-combustible and B is the highest class an organic material such as a laminate can reach. It is determined largely by the Single Burning Item test to EN 13823, which measures the rate at which heat is released and how much is released in total.

The s-number is smoke. s1 is the lowest of three, meaning very limited smoke production. In an evacuation this often matters more than the main class, because smoke stops people finding an exit long before flame reaches them.

The d-number is flaming droplets. d0 means none were produced within the first 600 seconds. Droplets spread fire downward and start secondary fires, which is why the figure is reported separately rather than folded into the main class.

The numbers behind the letters

The classification is a summary of measured values, and the values are worth reading. For the fire-retardant compact grade, the Single Burning Item test records a fire growth rate index (FIGRA) of 26.1 W/s against a 120 W/s limit for Class B, total heat release of 2.4 MJ against 7.5 MJ, and total smoke production of 27.7 m² against 50 m².

Those margins tell you something a letter cannot: how much headroom there is. A product that just scrapes into a class behaves differently in a real fire from one that clears it several times over.

Grade and thickness are part of the classification

This is where specifications go wrong. Fire performance is a property of a specific grade, tested at a specific thickness. The standard grade and the fire-retardant grade of the same product are different materials with different behaviour, and only one of them carries the classification.

For exterior compact, every published value was measured on 6 mm panels — the data sheet states this explicitly. A 10 mm panel of the same grade has not been tested at that thickness, and the classification cannot simply be assumed to carry across.

Which is why a fire classification should never appear as a badge on a product page. It belongs in a row that names the product, the grade, the thickness, the standard and the report.

What a panel test does not tell you

A reaction-to-fire classification describes the panel as tested. It does not certify a construction.

A ventilated façade is a system: panel, substructure, cavity, insulation, cavity barriers and fixings. The cavity in particular changes fire behaviour, because it can act as a flue. A panel classified B-s1 d0 mounted on an untested substructure produces an untested façade, and in most jurisdictions the assessment that matters is of the whole build-up, often to a large-scale test rather than to EN 13501-1 alone.

Ask for the panel classification and, separately, for whatever system evidence exists. Treating the first as if it were the second is the error that fire engineers look for first.

Other standards you will meet

Outside the European system, exterior compact carries results under ASTM E84 (Class A / Class I), BS 476 Parts 6 and 7 (Class 1), and UL 94 (V-0). These measure different things under different conditions and do not convert into each other. A Class A result under ASTM E84 is not the same statement as B-s1 d0, and quoting them as equivalents in a tender invites a challenge.

Common questions

Is B-s1, d0 good enough for a façade?+−

It depends entirely on the building: height, use and national regulation decide the required class, and many jurisdictions require A2 or better above a certain height. B-s1 d0 is the best classification achievable by a laminate panel. Whether it satisfies your project is a regulatory question, and the façade system — not the panel alone — is what gets assessed.

Does the standard grade have a fire classification too?+−

No. The classification applies to the fire-retardant grade only. Specifying the standard grade and quoting the fire-retardant classification is the single most common error in this area.

Can I get the full fire test report?+−

Fire reports are supplied on request rather than published for download. Ask through the quotation form and state the product, grade and thickness you intend to specify, so the right report is sent.
