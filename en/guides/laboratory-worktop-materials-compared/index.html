Laboratory worktops compared: compact laminate, epoxy resin, stainless steel or solid surface

Laboratory planners choose a bench by the chemistry on it. The design guidance of the US National Institutes of Health and of Yale University shows where each material belongs, and why the phenolic panels American laboratories use belong to the same family as European compact laminate.

7 min read·Updated Nov 20, 2026

In short

  • What US laboratory planners call phenolic resin is compact laminate in a laboratory grade. The NIH says such panels compare favourably with epoxy for most laboratory applications, and Yale's design standard gives them the same 50-year life and the same chemical-lab use as epoxy.
  • Compact wins on handling: about 28 kg/m² at 19 mm against about 59 kg/m² for a 25 mm epoxy top, cut with carbide tools and changed on site, where epoxy needs diamond blades and a workshop. Epoxy, ceramic and steel remain the choice for sustained aggressive chemistry, open flame or constant wash-down.
  • Rushil's compact is tested to SEFA 3-2020, with most results at Level 0 or 1. Send your reagent list with the enquiry: the answer that counts is the result for your own chemicals.

How this guide was put together

This guide draws on two bulletins that the Office of Research Facilities of the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) published for its Design Requirements Manual in February and March 2015, on phenolic resin panels and on epoxy resin benchtops; on the laboratory casework section of Yale University's design standard, updated in June 2025; and on Rushil's data sheets and its SEFA test summary. American institutions are a useful reference because they publish their reasoning; a European project still decides by its own reagent list and its own rules.

Phenolic resin is compact laminate by another name

American laboratory planners speak of phenolic resin panels. The NIH describes a cellulose fibre-reinforced phenolic resin core, monolithic and impervious to water, with its surface laminated in the factory under heat and pressure using thermosetting resins. That is the make-up of what Europe calls compact laminate, or compact HPL.

The difference that matters is the grade. General-purpose panels, the NIH notes, have long been used in furniture, casework and exterior cladding; laboratory-specific panels are a more recent development, formulated to be chemical and heat resistant, in a limited choice of colours such as black, white and greys. Its verdict is that their chemical and heat-resistant properties compare favourably with epoxy and other benchtop materials for most laboratory applications.

A laminate bonded to a board is a different product. The NIH treats it as a light-duty option because it can delaminate, whereas the core of a phenolic panel has no board in it to swell, so a cut edge needs no special treatment.

Which material for which laboratory

Yale's design standard puts the choice in a single table and tells designers to select by the use of the laboratory and an evaluation of the chemicals. It gives each material an expected life in years and a type of use.

Phenolic resin composite and epoxy resin share the same life and the same use. At the other end, the Yale School of Medicine allows plastic laminate only in limited applications with little moisture and no caustic reagents, and even then wants epoxy at sink cut-outs.

  • Plastic laminate: 20 years; offices, electronics labs, dry activities.
  • Chemical-resistant plastic laminate: 20 years; clinical labs.
  • Solid surfacing: 50 years; clinical labs and some chemical labs.
  • Phenolic resin composite: 50 years; chemical labs.
  • Epoxy resin, 25 mm: 50 years; chemical labs.
  • Stainless steel, type 302/304: listed for life; wet labs with frequent cleaning and high durability.

Epoxy: the benchmark, and its price

The NIH calls epoxy the workhorse of chemical-intensive laboratories and the most commonly used benchtop surface. It is an oven-cured mixture of epoxy resin, silica, a hardener and fillers, the same all the way through, so scratches do not reduce its chemical resistance. It is non-flammable, resistant to high temperatures and can be moulded into sinks and backsplashes.

The same bulletin lists what that costs. At about 12 lb per square foot, roughly 59 kg/m² for the standard 25 mm sheet, epoxy is substantially heavier than the alternatives. It has to be cut with diamond-tipped blades, preferably water-cooled, and mostly in a workshop, which makes it dearer to fabricate and harder to change later. Its material cost is higher, and colours other than black add 15 to 45 %. And although it resists high temperatures, it can crack from thermal shock if exposed to liquid nitrogen or other cryogens; the NIH singles out dry ice placed in epoxy sinks.

Where compact pulls ahead

Weight comes first. At 1.47 g/cm³, the density in Rushil's compact data sheets, a 19 mm panel weighs about 28 kg/m², less than half a 25 mm epoxy top. That counts on mobile and adjustable benches, in transport and on site.

Then workability. The NIH notes that phenolic panels can be worked with standard carbide-tipped tools and fixed with wood screws, so they can be fitted and modified on site, and that their core edges can stay exposed and allow under-mount sinks. Thinner panels extend the same material to back and side splashes, divider panels, utility enclosures, casework and lockers.

Long benches need joints with either material: the NIH gives about 1.8 × 2.4 m as the largest epoxy sheet and up to about 1.8 × 3 m for phenolic panels. Rushil's compact programme runs to 1830 × 4330 mm, which can move a joint further along the bench; confirm the format for the grade you specify.

Where compact is not the answer

The NIH's own line is a good one: phenolic panels belong in areas not appropriate for plastic laminate, but where the durability of epoxy is not required. They approach epoxy's chemical resistance without being monolithic in the way epoxy is, so treat a deep scratch as a change to the surface that was tested.

Sustained work with concentrated acids, strong solvents or open flame remains the territory of epoxy, ceramic or stainless steel, and steel is Yale's choice for wet labs that are cleaned constantly. Phenolic sinks are not made, so a compact bench takes an epoxy or stainless steel sink.

School science labs: what UK guidance asks of a bench

For school laboratories, the most detailed guidance we found comes from CLEAPSS, the advisory service for school science in the UK. Its guide G14, Designing and Planning Laboratories, published in May 2009, expects benches to serve for well over 20 years of spills, daily wiping, hot glassware, knocks and stools, and its planning checklist names three suitable bench tops: wood, stone-filled resin or solid laminate.

On laminates, G14 draws the same line as the NIH. Solid laminates, with a phenolic layer 12 to 20 mm thick, cannot be degraded by water ingress, whereas laminate glued to MDF or chipboard should be avoided in laboratories, because water at the edges and around sinks softens the glue and lifts the surface. G14 asks for at least 16 mm where a laminate has no board beneath it, recommends dark matt finishes for practical benches and notes that laminate benches are widely used in schools because they are relatively cheap. Rushil's TOPAZ and VAULT compact are documented from 10 to 18 mm, so an 18 mm top clears that minimum.

Mineral-filled acrylic or polyester resins, often offered to schools as polyester worktops, are usually a thin layer on an MDF base. In CLEAPSS's tests they took a hot crucible better than laminate and can be repaired by sanding, while stools placed on them caused some chipping. On cost, G14 gives no ranking: ask suppliers to quote different materials at the same thickness and compare like with like. One caution from the same tests applies to every laminate: a red-hot crucible or a Bunsen flame held on it leaves a permanent mark, so use heat-proof mats, and choose epoxy where open flames and hot crucibles are routine.

Common questions

Is phenolic resin the same as compact laminate?+−

In make-up, yes: both are cellulose-reinforced phenolic cores pressed with a surface under heat and pressure. Laboratory-grade panels are formulated for chemical and heat resistance, so ask for a SEFA report rather than assuming any compact panel qualifies.

Is compact as chemical resistant as epoxy?+−

The NIH says laboratory-grade phenolic panels compare favourably with epoxy for most laboratory applications and approach its chemical resistance. That is not a blanket equivalence: read the results reagent by reagent, and keep epoxy, ceramic or steel for sustained concentrated acids, strong solvents or open flame.

What sink goes with a compact laboratory worktop?+−

Phenolic resin sinks are not available, the NIH notes, so epoxy or stainless steel sinks are usual, often under-mounted because the core edge needs no special treatment. Do not put dry ice in an epoxy sink: thermal shock can crack it.

How long does a laboratory worktop last?+−

Yale's design standard expects 20 years from plastic laminate, 50 years from solid surfacing, phenolic resin composite and epoxy resin, and lists stainless steel for life. These are planning figures, not guarantees.

What worktop material is best for a school science lab?+−

UK guidance from CLEAPSS lists wood, stone-filled resin and solid laminate as suitable bench tops. Solid laminate, that is compact at 16 mm or more, cannot be degraded by water ingress and is widely used because it is relatively affordable; use heat-proof mats for red-hot crucibles, and epoxy where open flames are routine.
