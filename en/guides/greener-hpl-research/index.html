Greener HPL: lignin, sugar-based resins and a second life for waste

HPL owes much of its toughness to fossil-based phenolic resin, and making it takes energy. Laboratories in Europe, the Americas and Asia are working on both ends of the problem. This is how far they have got.

7 min read·Updated Oct 22, 2026

In short

  • The honest starting point: a 2025 gate-to-gate life-cycle assessment found HPL production had the greatest impact of four decorative surface materials.
  • Laboratories are replacing fossil phenol: 20 % hardwood lignin showed no significant loss of properties, 40 % pine lignin basically met EN 438-2 for outdoor use, and a sugar-based resin met compact requirements at pilot scale.
  • Waste has routes too — sanding dust in wood-plastic composites, residue in recycled polypropylene, gasification into hydrogen-rich gas — but none is yet standard industrial practice.

How this review was put together

This article summarises eight peer-reviewed papers: a life-cycle assessment of decorative surface materials from the Chinese Academy of Forestry (Lao and Zhu, Journal of Building Engineering, 2025); four studies on the resin and the paper, from BOKU University and the Wood K plus research centre in Austria (Ghorbani et al., European Journal of Wood and Wood Products, 2018; Billich et al., ACS Omega, 2025; Billich et al., Journal of Applied Polymer Science, 2025) and from Argentina (Taverna et al., Waste and Biomass Valorization, 2019); and three on what to do with HPL waste, from Brazil and Canada (Lopes et al., Next Energy, 2024), Nanjing Forestry University (Huang et al., Waste Management, 2022) and the Federal University of São Carlos (Pires et al., Polymer Testing, 2019). Most of it is laboratory or pilot work, and none of it describes how Rushil's panels are made.

The honest starting point

HPL is layers of paper bonded with phenol–formaldehyde resin in the core and melamine–formaldehyde on the faces, and its excellent properties stem primarily from the stability of the fossil-based phenolic resin, the Austrian team writes. That has a cost. A 2025 gate-to-gate life-cycle assessment of four decorative surface materials, printed paper, impregnated paper, HPL and PVC decorative film, found the HPL production process to have the greatest environmental impact of the four across 13 midpoint indicators, with printed paper the least.

A gate-to-gate assessment stops at the factory gate: it does not weigh how long each surface lasts in use, and a paper finish and a compact panel do not do the same job. It does explain why the industry is looking for alternatives.

Recycled kraft paper is already here

The laminate industry already makes increasing use of recycled kraft paper to reduce its footprint, the Austrian researchers note. Recycled paper behaves differently, with a lower surface free energy and more difference between its two sides, which they attribute to higher lignin and extractive content and to changes in the fibres. In their tests, bio-based resins still wetted it about as well as commercial phenolic resin, though they penetrated it differently.

Lignin in place of phenol

Lignin, the polymer that binds wood fibres together and a by-product of pulp mills, contains phenolic units that react with formaldehyde much as phenol does. At BOKU, researchers replaced 40 % of the phenol in the core resin with pine kraft lignin: the laminates basically met the EN 438-2 requirements for outdoor use in water absorption, thickness swelling, flexural strength, modulus and impact. A version with spruce lignosulfonate failed some of the tests because it took up more water, especially under rapid changes of temperature and humidity.

In Argentina, working with a local HPL manufacturer, a team replaced up to 20 % of the phenol with hardwood kraft lignin and found no significant differences in mechanical and thermal properties; the modified laminates even resisted water better, which the authors put down to lignin's hydrophobic nature.

A resin made from sugars

The Austrian group then went further, with no phenol at all: a resin made from carbohydrate-based compounds, fructose and its derivative HMF, cross-linked with an amine. Recycled kraft paper was impregnated with it on a pilot-scale line and pressed into a laboratory HPL panel. Its mechanical properties met the industry requirements for compact panels and, in the cross direction, even exceeded conventional phenolic HPL. Its water resistance, the authors write, still needs improvement.

A second life for offcuts and dust

Making and machining HPL leaves offcuts and sanding dust. At Nanjing Forestry University, researchers made wood-plastic composites from 60 % decorative HPL sanding dust and waste polypropylene food pails: tensile strength of 35–42 MPa, flexural strength up to 43–46 MPa, impact strength of 5–6 kJ/m², formaldehyde emission of no more than 1.53 mg/m³ and slightly better flame retardancy.

In São Carlos, HPL residue powder used as a filler in recycled polypropylene dispersed well. At 40 % it raised the tensile and flexural modulus and the heat deflection temperature, while tensile and flexural strength fell; the authors suggest such blends for crates, pallets, chairs and vehicle interior parts.

Waste into hydrogen-rich gas

Where material recycling ends, energy recovery can begin. A Brazilian–Canadian team gasified HPL waste in a downdraft gasifier with air and steam, and notes that no energy-recovery studies existed for this waste before. At 900 °C and a low equivalence ratio, the gas contained 43.8 vol% hydrogen, the syngas fraction reached 58 vol% and the lower heating value 7.4 MJ/Nm³. The thermosetting resins helped rather than hindered: with them, the syngas was richer, with 62.3 vol% hydrogen and a hydrogen-to-carbon-monoxide ratio of 2.4.

What this means today

Two honest conclusions. The research is real but early: lignin substitution of 20–40 % meets the standards in the laboratory, a phenol-free resin meets compact mechanical requirements at pilot scale, and waste routes work in extruders and reactors, but none of this is yet standard industrial practice. And the long service life of HPL is part of the equation that a gate-to-gate assessment leaves out: a surface that does not need replacing avoids a second production. That argument needs data of its own, and we do not present it here as proven.

Common questions

Does HPL production have a high environmental impact?+−

In a 2025 gate-to-gate life-cycle assessment of four decorative surface materials, HPL production had the greatest impact, followed by PVC film, impregnated paper and printed paper. The assessment covers production only, not service life.

Can the phenol in HPL be replaced?+−

Partly, in the laboratory: up to 20 % hardwood kraft lignin showed no significant loss of properties, and 40 % pine kraft lignin basically met the EN 438-2 requirements for outdoor use. A phenol-free, carbohydrate-based resin met the mechanical requirements for compact panels at pilot scale but needs better water resistance.

What can be done with HPL waste?+−

Research shows three routes: sanding dust as a filler in wood-plastic composites, HPL residue as a filler in recycled polypropylene, and gasification into hydrogen-rich syngas. All three have been demonstrated at laboratory or pilot scale.
