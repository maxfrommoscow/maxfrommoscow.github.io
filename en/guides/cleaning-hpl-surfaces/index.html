Cleaning HPL and compact surfaces: what the industry guidance says

HPL needs little care, but it is not indestructible. Europe's laminate manufacturers publish one cleaning guide for the whole material. This is what it says, stain by stain.

5 min read·Updated Oct 22, 2026

In short

  • HPL's almost pore-free surface needs little care: warm water and a soft cloth, then dry; for heavier dirt, a non-abrasive cleaner rinsed off completely.
  • It resists alcohol- and aldehyde-based disinfectants such as 70 % ethanol, and limescale comes off with a warm 10 % acetic or citric acid solution left for about ten minutes.
  • Avoid scouring powder and abrasive pads. Rushil's interior compact reaches grade 5, the top of the scale, for staining, scratching and dry heat in the EN 438 tests.

How this guide was put together

This article draws on two technical leaflets of proHPL, the decorative laminates group of the German plastics industry association pro-K — 'Reinigung von HPL' (December 2020) and 'Chemische Beständigkeit und hygienische Eigenschaften von HPL' (January 2020) — and on 'Cleaning of HPL surfaces' (December 2017) by the International Committee of the Decorative Laminates Industry. They apply to all HPL to EN 438, compact included. Each point is attributed, and the sources are listed at the end.

Why HPL is easy to clean

HPL is a thermosetting composite of resin and paper with an almost pore-free surface, proHPL explains, which is why it needs little care and cleans easily. The ICDLI adds a caveat worth keeping in mind: the material is extremely durable, but it is not indestructible.

Everyday cleaning

The routine is plain: warm water, then dry with a paper towel or a soft cloth. For more stubborn dirt, proHPL lists detergent, glass cleaner and liquid or hard soap that leaves no greasy film — anything without abrasive particles. Let the cleaner act as long as the soiling needs, rinse with water and dry; repeat if necessary.

Two details make the difference. Rinse off every trace of the cleaner, or it dries into streaks, and dry with a clean, absorbent cloth. A non-abrasive sponge, a microfibre cloth or a soft plastic brush helps; abrasive cleaners do not, because they damage the surface.

Stain by stain

Limescale: a warm 10 % solution of acetic or citric acid, left for ten minutes, then rinsed thoroughly with warm water; after a commercial descaler, rinse at once. Wax or paraffin: lift it first with a plastic or wooden spatula so the surface is not scratched, then lay blotting paper over what remains and touch it briefly with a hot iron on its lowest setting.

Paint, lacquer and glue: fresh water-based residues come off with warm water, dried ones with ethanol, acetone or white spirit; solvent-based ones with the same solvents, given time. Two-part adhesives and lacquers, such as epoxy or polyurethane, must be removed immediately with a suitable solvent — once cured, proHPL warns, they cannot be removed without trace. Silicone or polyurethane sealant: scrape first, then use a remover as its maker directs. After any solvent, wash with warm water and dry.

Chemicals, disinfectants and heat

HPL resists most household chemicals, but spills should be wiped up at once, and long contact with aggressive cleaners — toilet, oven and metal cleaners in particular — avoided. Whether a chemical marks the surface depends on its concentration, its pH, the contact time and the temperature, proHPL notes.

For hygiene, ethanol removes most common household microorganisms, and HPL resists disinfectants based on alcohols, such as 70 % ethanol, and on aldehydes. It withstands short exposure to temperatures up to 180 °C; extreme heat can discolour or char it, so hot equipment belongs on a heat-resistant base such as a ceramic tile.

What not to use

Summed up from the same leaflets: no scouring powder, abrasive pads or cleaners with abrasive particles; no long contact with aggressive cleaners; scraping only with plastic or wooden tools; and no delay with two-part adhesives, which have to come off before they cure.

Reading this against the Rushil range

The surface these leaflets describe is the one EN 438 tests. For Rushil's interior compact, the TOPAZ and VAULT data sheets report grade 5, the top of the scale, for resistance to staining in all three groups of EN 438-2 test agents (required: 5 for groups 1 and 2, 4 for group 3), 5 for resistance to scratching (required: 2 on smooth and 3 on textured surfaces) and 5 for resistance to dry heat (required: 4). Rushil has not published a care document of its own; the industry guidance above applies to HPL to EN 438 in general.

Common questions

What is the best everyday cleaner for HPL?+−

Warm water and a soft cloth, then dry. For heavier dirt, a normal non-abrasive cleaner such as detergent or glass cleaner, rinsed off completely so it leaves no streaks (proHPL, ICDLI).

Can HPL be cleaned with acetone or disinfectant?+−

Yes. proHPL names ethanol, acetone and white spirit for paint and adhesive residues, and lists HPL as resistant to alcohol- and aldehyde-based disinfectants such as 70 % ethanol. Wash with warm water afterwards.

How do I remove limescale from HPL?+−

With a warm 10 % solution of acetic or citric acid, left for about ten minutes and then rinsed off thoroughly with warm water (proHPL).
