HPL in healthcare: what infection-control research has measured

Hospitals treat surfaces as part of infection control. A handful of peer-reviewed studies have put high-pressure laminate under the microscope. This is what they found, the favourable and the less so.

7 min read·Updated Oct 22, 2026

In short

  • In a Baylor University study no MRSA was found on HPL after 24 hours, while stainless steel and acrylic solid surface still carried bacteria; two hospital disinfectants cleared HPL completely.
  • HPL held its surface best under wear — its roughness changed by 1.7 % after abrasion simulating six years of use, the smallest change of five materials — and laminate passed the least bacteria from surface to skin, 1.5 %.
  • Untreated laminate does not disinfect itself; antimicrobial activity comes from treated surfaces. Rushil's TOPAZ and VAULT reduce bacteria and viruses by more than 99.99 %.

How this review was put together

This article summarises four open-access, peer-reviewed publications: a laboratory study of hospital surface materials from Baylor University in the US (Harris et al., HERD, 2021), a study of bacterial transfer and cleaning from the SP Technical Research Institute of Sweden (Arinder et al., PLOS ONE, 2016), a study of antimicrobial HPL from the University of Aveiro in Portugal (Magina et al., Materials, 2016), and a review of how long pathogens survive on surfaces from Ruhr University Bochum (Wißmann et al., Microorganisms, 2021). All four describe laboratory work on test samples, not trials in working hospitals, and we say so where it matters. The papers are listed at the end.

Why the surface matters

Contaminated surfaces in patient rooms play a recognised part in spreading hospital pathogens such as MRSA, which can stay viable on surfaces for up to 14 days, the Baylor team notes in its review of the literature. That makes the material of a bed table, a cabinet front or a wall panel an infection-control question, not only a design one.

Five materials, six simulated years

The Baylor study set HPL beside four other hospital surfaces: brushed stainless steel, an acrylic solid surface, a solid surface containing cupric oxide, and a copper–nickel sheet. Samples were tested new and after abrasion on a Taber tester set to simulate six years of normal wear, and every surface was examined under an atomic force microscope.

New HPL had a surface roughness of 236 nm, close to the acrylic solid surface (238 nm) and rougher than brushed steel (113 nm). After the simulated wear it measured 240 nm, a change of 1.7 % and the smallest of the five. The copper sheet roughened by 71.6 %; the cupric-oxide surface smoothed by 47.8 %. The study also records that every material except steel lost significant mass under abrasion, HPL included, and that worn HPL looked flaky under the electron microscope. The surface wore, but its roughness barely moved.

MRSA after 24 hours, and two disinfectants

Each sample was inoculated with MRSA and held at 35 °C. After two hours every surface still carried more colonies than could be counted. After 24 hours none were found on HPL, nor on the two copper-based surfaces, while stainless steel and the acrylic solid surface still carried 52 to 147 colony-forming units. After 72 hours every surface was clear. Both disinfectants tested, a bleach-based healthcare cleaner and a newer decontaminant, eradicated MRSA on all five materials, with no difference between them.

The authors are careful about the limits. Colonies were counted in categories rather than exactly, no soil load was used, and the work was done in a laboratory. They credit an active reduction of MRSA only to the copper-based surfaces. For HPL the result is best read as: no worse than stainless steel, and fully disinfectable with standard products.

Laminate and steel: survival in days

The 2021 review from Bochum collects decades of survival data. In one study it cites, by Webster and colleagues, Staphylococcus aureus survived 9 days on laminate and 10 on stainless steel, Pseudomonas aeruginosa 4 and 5 days, and Acinetobacter baumannii 11 and 12 days; the review notes that the starting bacterial load was not specified. On these figures laminate behaves much like steel: neither surface disinfects itself, and both need cleaning.

Hands, surfaces and the wet cloth

The Swedish study measured how much Staphylococcus aureus moves between artificial skin and seven materials. From contaminated skin to the surface, transfer was similar for every material except the textile, 33 to 39 %, with melamine laminate at 38.2 %. In the other direction, from a contaminated surface to skin, laminate passed on the least: 1.5 %, against 5.3 % for stainless steel and 9.4 % for ceramic tile. The authors found the differences between materials not statistically significant.

Cleaning is where the laminate came off worst. Wiped with three cleaning agents and two kinds of cloth, the laminate sample, a commercial melamine laminate, was the hardest of the three test surfaces to clean. But the largest single factor in the whole experiment was not the material. It was how wet the cloth was: a well-moistened wipe removed significantly more bacteria than a drier one. For anyone writing a cleaning protocol, that is the practical lesson.

Antimicrobial activity built into the resin

Researchers at the University of Aveiro went a step further and bound an antimicrobial polymer, polyhexamethylene biguanide (PHMB), into the melamine resin of the HPL surface. At 1.0 % of the resin it stopped Listeria innocua and Escherichia coli from growing; at 2.4 % it killed them. The laminates still met EN 438 for boiling water, steam, dry heat, impact and staining, but scratch resistance fell to grade 3 against the required 4: every additive has a cost.

Commercial compact grades take a related route. Rushil's TOPAZ and VAULT data sheets report a bacterial reduction above 99.99 % to JIS Z 2801 and a viral reduction above 99.99 % to ISO 21702. A separate article on this site explains what those tests measure, and what they cannot prove.

What this means for a healthcare specification

Read together, the studies support four points. HPL can be disinfected as reliably as stainless steel with standard hospital products. Its surface roughness stays practically unchanged after simulated years of wear. Untreated, it does not disinfect itself, and bacteria can persist on it for days, as on steel. And the cleaning method, with enough liquid, the right agent and enough contact time, matters at least as much as the material. One point lies outside these studies but matters in practice: joints and edges. Compact has a solid edge that needs no edge banding, which leaves fewer seams for dirt and moisture.

Common questions

Is HPL antibacterial by itself?+−

Untreated HPL does not disinfect itself: in the studies reviewed here bacteria survived on laminate for hours to days, much as on stainless steel. Antimicrobial activity comes from treating the surface, as in the University of Aveiro research and in compact grades tested to JIS Z 2801, and it supports cleaning rather than replacing it.

Can HPL be cleaned with hospital disinfectants?+−

Yes. In the Baylor University study a bleach-based healthcare cleaner and a second disinfectant both eradicated MRSA from HPL, as from stainless steel and solid surface. Follow the laminate maker's guidance on concentration and contact time.

Does an HPL surface get harder to clean as it wears?+−

In the Baylor study HPL's surface roughness changed by 1.7 % after abrasion simulating six years of use, the smallest change of the five materials tested, although like every material except steel it lost measurable mass.
